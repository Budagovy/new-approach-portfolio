THE PROBLEM, THE SOLUTION & MY ROLE

From a broad brief
to a complete product.

A founder and two team members brought me an early vision for flexible workspace booking. I led product design from defining the experience to preparing it for development, working with the team through to production.

THE PROBLEM
Flexibility without losing connection.
Employees wanted less commuting and more choice, but remote work could feel isolating. Companies wanted to reduce underused office space while preserving connection and collaboration.

THE SOLUTION
Choose a place. Plan who to join.
SecondOffice brings workspace discovery, booking and reservation management into one mobile experience. People can see who plans to be there and invite others to share a workday.

MY END-TO-END OWNERSHIP
I analyzed research data and competitor findings, defined flows, designed the UI and prototype, tested with users, presented decisions to stakeholders and checked feasibility with developers. Research also led me to challenge the booking-only brief and propose a social layer.

COMPETITIVE ANALYSIS & KEY INSIGHT

Booking was the starting point.
People changed the brief.

I benchmarked hybrid workspace products to compare shared capabilities and identify an opportunity to connect booking with belonging.
The original direction focused on reserving a desk. User conversations led me to expand it: knowing who planned to be there could also influence the choice of workspace.

LEADING THE PRODUCT WITH STAKEHOLDERS & DEVELOPERS

From product definition
to delivery.

I shaped the core flows, presented design decisions to stakeholders and checked feasibility with developers. One key decision was the social layer: when the founder questioned its added cost and complexity, I used the research to explain its value. We agreed to prototype and test it before development.

THE DESIGN CHALLENGE
Keep booking lightweight.
Make connection possible.
The social layer added product complexity and development effort. I had to balance individual productivity with connection on a small mobile screen.
I carried this balance across the core flows, UI and prototype, aligning product priorities with stakeholders and feasibility with developers.

KEY SOLUTION / BOOKING

One clear place to start.
Three steps toward a desk.

I designed the core flow for the morning, before leaving for work: a prominent Where action, workspace discovery and a space-detail view.

01 Start with Where
02 Explore places & people
03 Review a workspace

A CLEAR ENTRY POINT
The morning task needed an obvious starting action. Inspired by Shazam, I gave Where visual priority. This was a design intention, not a measured speed improvement.
PEOPLE IN THE CHOICE
The research connected flexibility with belonging. Showing people on the map brought that context into workspace discovery, before choosing a place.
CONTEXT BEFORE BOOKING
The detail view combines amenities, people and price. The booking action sits beside the price, keeping cost visible at the point of commitment.

KEY SOLUTION / GETTOGETHER

A shared workday,
without all the back and forth.

Choose a workspace.
Bring people into the plan.
The workspace is the shared destination: people drag contacts into its circle, then continue to invite them. This makes the proposed group visible around a place. Connections can extend beyond an immediate team.
My hypothesis: visible work plans could reduce the need for several conversations and delayed replies in the morning. Visibility helps with the choice; an invitation makes it actionable.

VALIDATION, OUTCOME & LEARNING

What I learned
from testing and delivery.

PROTOTYPE VALIDATION
Employees from different companies tested the prototype. Feedback highlighted the simplicity of booking and the appeal of GetTogether. Testing covered the app as a whole and supported moving into development.

PRODUCTION OUTCOME
The app, including the social features shown here, reached production and was used outside testing. It was live for a limited period before the company closed. I do not have verified adoption or retention figures to report.

WHAT I LEARNED
Designing the transaction was only part
of designing the workday.
The project taught me to consider both the individual task and its social context, and to make a new direction tangible and testable when its added scope raises questions.
